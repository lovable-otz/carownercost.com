How much car can I afford on my salary?

Start from the payment, not the sticker price. Pick the share of gross monthly income you are comfortable spending on the loan, turn that into a maximum loan at your APR and term, then add your down payment and trade-in. That total is the price, including financed tax and fees, that the payment can carry.

The calculator runs the loan formula in reverse: maximum loan = payment × (1 − (1 + r)^−n) ÷ r, with r as the monthly rate and n as the months.

Here is what that gives at a 10% payment share, 6% APR, 48 months and $4,000 down. These are illustrations, not recommendations:

The rate matters as much as the income. Finance companies averaged 6.3% on new-car loans in June 2026, with an average amount financed of $41,705 over 67 months (Federal Reserve G.19, September 8, 2026). A longer term would raise every price in the table, but it also raises the interest you pay. You can see that trade-off in the car payment calculator.

Remember that the price is what the loan and down payment cover in total. If your state’s sales tax and fees are financed, the car’s own sticker price has to be lower than this figure.

What share of income should go to a car?

There is no official limit. One widely quoted guideline is the 20/4/10 rule, which Chase describes as 20% down, a loan of four years or less, and total transportation costs of no more than 10% of gross monthly income (Chase, undated). In that version the 10% covers the payment, insurance, maintenance and fuel together.

That is stricter than the default in this calculator, where the percentage applies to the payment alone. If you want to follow the 20/4/10 version, estimate your insurance, fuel and upkeep first, subtract them from 10% of your income, and enter what is left as the payment share.

Real spending runs higher than 10% for many households. In the 2024 Consumer Expenditure Survey, the average household had $104,207 of pre-tax income and spent $13,318 on transportation, 17.0% of total spending (BLS, released December 19, 2025). That $13,318 includes $5,337 of net vehicle purchases and $4,206 of other vehicle expenses.

A rule of thumb is a guideline, not a test you pass or fail. Consider your other debts, savings goals and how stable your income is, and treat the result here as an estimate to compare against real loan offers.

The costs beyond the payment

The loan payment is often less than half of what a car costs to own. AAA’s 2026 Your Driving Costs study puts the average new vehicle at $12,863 a year, about $1,071.92 a month, over five years and 75,000 miles (AAA, September 15, 2026). Depreciation alone averaged $4,422 a year, the biggest single cost.

Budget for these on top of the payment:

  • Insurance. Vehicle insurance spending rose 12.3% in 2024, one of the faster-rising items in the BLS survey (released December 19, 2025).
  • Fuel or charging. Price a year of driving with the gas cost calculator or, for an EV, the EV charging cost calculator.
  • Maintenance and repairs, which grow as the car ages.
  • Registration, title and taxes. The CFPB notes these are set by government and can’t be negotiated.

The CFPB also suggests factoring in insurance, registration, maintenance, gas and repairs when you compare loan offers, since these can differ between new and used cars (CFPB, reviewed January 30, 2024). A cheaper car with higher running costs can end up costing about the same each month.

Frequently asked questions

How much should I spend on a car if I make $70,000 a year?

At a 10% payment share, $70,000 a year supports about $583 a month. With a 6% APR over 48 months and $4,000 down, that points to a car price of about $28,839 including financed tax and fees. A lower share, higher rate or smaller down payment lowers that figure.

How much should I spend on a car if I make $100,000 a year?

Using the same example assumptions (10% of gross income, 6% APR, 48 months, $4,000 down), $100,000 a year supports a payment of about $833 and a car price near $39,484. If you follow the stricter 20/4/10 version, insurance and fuel come out of that 10% too, so the price would be lower.

How much car can I afford on a $60,000 salary?

About $25,290 under the example assumptions: a $500 monthly payment (10% of $5,000 gross), 6% APR, 48 months and $4,000 down. Enter your own rate and term in the calculator, because a higher APR shrinks the loan the same payment can carry.

How much do I need to make to comfortably afford a $30,000 car?

Roughly $73,300 a year under the example assumptions. With $4,000 down, you would finance $26,000; at 6% over 48 months that is about $610.61 a month, which is 10% of about $73,273 of gross income. Different rates, terms or rules of thumb change the answer.
