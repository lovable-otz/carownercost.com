Car Payment Calculator

How is a car payment calculated?

Your payment comes from three numbers: the amount financed, the monthly interest rate (APR divided by 12) and the number of months. The standard amortization formula spreads principal and interest into equal payments, so early payments carry more interest and later ones pay down more principal.

The amount financed is where most estimates go wrong. This calculator builds it the way a dealer contract does:

  1. Start with the vehicle price.
  2. Add sales tax, on the full price or on the price minus your trade-in, depending on your state.
  3. Add title, registration and dealer fees if you roll them into the loan.
  4. Subtract your down payment and trade-in value.

The payment formula is amount financed × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)^−n), where r is the monthly rate and n is the number of months. Multiply the payment by n and subtract the amount financed to get total interest.

Your lender has to show the same building blocks before you sign. The Truth-in-Lending disclosure lists the APR, the finance charge, the amount financed and the total of payments (CFPB, reviewed March 8, 2024). Match those four lines against this estimate. If the dealer’s amount financed is higher than yours, look for add-ons or fees you did not agree to.

For context on the rate box: commercial banks averaged 7.14% on 60-month new-car loans in the second quarter of 2026 (Federal Reserve G.19, September 8, 2026). Your rate depends on your credit and lender, so treat any APR here as an example and use the one on your own offer.

Worked example: a $35,000 car over 60 months

A $35,000 car with $5,000 down, a $3,000 trade-in, 6.25% sales tax on the price minus the trade-in, $500 of fees and a 6.5% APR over 60 months works out to about $577.20 a month. You would pay $5,132.08 in interest over the loan.

The 6.25% rate is not random. Texas charges motor vehicle sales tax at 6.25% of the sales price minus any trade-in allowance (Texas Comptroller). The calculator’s Texas default is 8.2%, the state rate plus the average local rate from the Tax Foundation 2026 midyear table, because the tool starts from general sales tax rates. Texas buyers should type 6.25% into the tax box; buyers elsewhere should check their own state’s vehicle rule.

Assumptions: fees are financed, the APR is an example rather than a quote, and there are no add-on products. Putting $5,000 more down in the same example ($24,500 financed) drops the payment to about $479.37 and interest to $4,262.24.

How does the loan term change what you pay?

A longer term lowers the monthly payment but raises total interest, and the CFPB notes that longer loans also raise the risk of owing more than the car is worth (CFPB, reviewed August 28, 2023). On the same $29,500 at 6.5%, stretching from 60 to 84 months saves about $139 a month and costs about $2,165 more in interest.

The CFPB’s own comparison of a $20,000 loan at 4.75% shows the same pattern: interest roughly doubles when the term goes from 36 to 72 months (CFPB, reviewed January 30, 2024). It also points out that terms of five years or less are often suggested. If the payment only fits at 72 or 84 months, consider testing a lower price in the car affordability calculator before you shop.

Does sales tax get added to the loan?

Usually, yes. Sales tax is part of the out-the-door price (FTC, updated July 2022), and unless you pay it in cash at signing it is added to the amount financed, so you pay interest on it too. In the worked example, $2,000 of tax adds about $39 to each monthly payment at 6.5% over 60 months.

Two state rules change the tax figure a lot:

  • Trade-in credit. Some states tax only the price minus your trade-in. In the example, taxing the full $35,000 at 6.25% instead would add $187.50 of tax. Untick the trade-in box if your state taxes the full price.
  • Vehicle-specific rates. Some states use a separate motor vehicle tax, cap the tax or skip local rates. The Texas 6.25% vehicle rate is one example.

You cannot bargain the tax down. The CFPB lists taxes, title and registration fees set by state and local government as items you can’t negotiate (CFPB, reviewed August 28, 2023); dealer fees such as documentation and delivery charges are negotiable.

To get the tax figure right before you plug it in here, use the car sales tax calculator or look up your state on the 2026 car sales tax by state table, then confirm with your state DMV or revenue department.

Frequently asked questions

What APR should I put in a car payment calculator?

Use the APR on a written offer from your bank, credit union or dealer. Without one, a recent average gives a rough starting point: finance companies charged 6.3% on new-car loans in June 2026, and banks averaged 7.14% on 60-month loans in the second quarter (Federal Reserve G.19). Your own rate can be higher or lower.

Does a bigger down payment lower my car payment?

Yes, every dollar down is a dollar you don’t finance. In the $35,000 example at 6.5% over 60 months, raising the down payment from $5,000 to $10,000 lowers the payment from $577.20 to about $479.37 and cuts total interest by about $870. The calculator updates both numbers as you type.

Why is the dealer’s payment different from this estimate?

The usual reasons are a different amount financed, a different APR or added products. Compare the amount financed, APR, finance charge and total of payments on the Truth-in-Lending disclosure (CFPB) with this estimate. Add-ons like extended warranties and gap coverage are optional and cost money (FTC).
