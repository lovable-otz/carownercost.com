EV Charging Cost Calculator

An EV charging cost calculator multiplies the electricity your car uses by your price per kWh. Driving 12,000 miles a year at 30 kWh per 100 miles takes 3,600 kWh; at the June 2026 U.S. residential average of 18.34¢ per kWh (EIA), home charging costs about $660 a year.

Updated September 20, 2026 · By Car Owner Cost

How much does it cost to charge an EV at home?

Home charging cost = miles ÷ 100 × the car’s kWh per 100 miles × your price per kWh. At the U.S. residential average of 18.34¢ (EIA Electric Power Monthly, June 2026, released August 26, 2026), a car using 30 kWh per 100 miles costs about 5.5 cents a mile, or $660.24 for 12,000 miles.

Your state’s electricity price moves the answer more than anything else. For the same 3,600 kWh a year:

EV vs gas: what a year of driving costs

With the defaults, a year of home charging costs $660.24. The same 12,000 miles in a 30 MPG gas car at this week’s U.S. average of $4.319 (EIA, week of September 14, 2026) costs $1,727.60. That is $1,067.36 a year less on fuel for the EV.

The gap depends on both prices. California pairs costly electricity with costly gas: at 34.74¢ and the state’s $5.827 weekly average, the EV costs $1,250.64 a year and the 30 MPG car $2,330.80. Switch the gas area to the Gulf Coast ($3.852) and the gas car drops to $1,540.80.

What this comparison leaves out:

  • Purchase price, insurance and maintenance, and depreciation, which the car depreciation calculator estimates at a rate you choose.
  • Public charging, which usually costs more per kWh than charging at home (see below).
  • Your real MPG, which you can measure from a fill-up with the MPG calculator.

All results are estimates for home charging only.

What does kWh per 100 miles mean?

kWh per 100 miles is how much electricity an EV needs to travel 100 miles, and it appears on the EPA window sticker. Lower is better: a car rated at 25 kWh/100 mi costs less to run than one rated at 35. The fueleconomy.gov label guide describes it as the kilowatt-hours the vehicle would use to go 100 miles.

EPA says its MPGe values include charging losses from the charging cable and the car’s on-board charger, which moves the measurement “to the outlet in the wall” (EPA, updated July 1, 2026). EPA also adjusts its EV test results to reflect air conditioning, cold weather and high-speed, aggressive driving. The calculator adds no further loss on top of the kWh figure you enter.

Cost per mile = kWh per 100 miles ÷ 100 × price per kWh. At 30 kWh/100 mi and 18.34¢, that is 5.5 cents a mile. The AFDC gives a similar example: 27 kWh per 100 miles at 10.7¢ is about 3 cents a mile.

Public fast charging vs home charging

Public DC fast charging usually costs far more per kWh than charging at home. Paren’s Q1 2026 industry report put the average U.S. fast-charging price at $0.53 per kWh excluding free chargers, and $0.49 including them, as reported by EVChargingStations.com on April 23, 2026. The June 2026 U.S. home average was 18.34¢.

At $0.53, the 3,600 kWh in the example would cost $1,908 a year, more than the $1,727.60 for a 30 MPG gas car at $4.319. Most drivers mix the two, so real costs usually land somewhere in between.

The calculator covers home charging only. To estimate a mix, enter a blended price: 80% of charging at home at 18.34¢ and 20% at $0.53 averages about 25.3¢ per kWh.

Fast-charging prices vary from station to station, so check the price shown on the charger or in its app before you plug in.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate the cost of charging an EV?

Multiply the kWh you use by your price per kWh. The kWh used is miles ÷ 100 × the car’s kWh per 100 miles from its window sticker. For 1,000 miles at 30 kWh/100 mi, that is 300 kWh, which costs $55.02 at 18.34¢. The rate on your own electric bill gives the closest estimate.

How much does it cost to charge an electric car to go 300 miles?

About $16.51 at home at the June 2026 U.S. average of 18.34¢ per kWh, for a car using 30 kWh per 100 miles. The trip takes 90 kWh. At California’s 34.74¢, the same 90 kWh costs about $31.27, and at a fast charger priced at $0.53 per kWh, about $47.70. A less efficient EV costs proportionally more.

Can I get a free EV home charger?

There is no general federal program that provides one. The IRS alternative fuel vehicle refueling property credit covered 30% of the cost, up to $1,000 per item, only for chargers placed in service at a main home in eligible census tracts from January 1, 2023, to June 30, 2026 (IRS page updated May 26, 2026). The AFDC says state and utility incentives may help, so ask your utility.

How much does charging an EV add to your electric bill?

About $55 a month in a typical example. Driving 12,000 miles a year at 30 kWh per 100 miles adds 3,600 kWh, or 300 kWh a month, which costs $55.02 at the June 2026 U.S. residential average of 18.34¢. Your figure scales with the miles you drive, your car’s efficiency and your utility’s rate.
