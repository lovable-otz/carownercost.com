Car Depreciation Calculator

How fast do cars lose value?

Depreciation is usually the biggest cost of owning a new car. AAA’s 2026 Your Driving Costs study found an average of $4,422 a year, based on five years and 75,000 miles, out of $12,863 in total yearly ownership costs (AAA, September 15, 2026). How fast your own car loses value depends on the model, mileage, condition and market.

This calculator uses declining balance: each year the car loses the same percentage of its value at the start of that year, so the dollar loss shrinks over time. The 15% default is an assumption for illustration, not a market figure.

How to estimate your own car’s depreciation rate

Use real listings. Find the current asking or sold prices for your model, year, trim and similar mileage, compare them with what the car cost new, and convert the drop into a yearly rate with this formula: rate = 1 − (today’s value ÷ original price)^(1 ÷ years). Enter that rate in the tool.

  1. Collect at least five listings for the same model and trim, three to five years old, with mileage near what you expect to drive.
  2. Take the middle price rather than the highest.
  3. Divide by the original price and apply the formula.

Illustration with made-up numbers: a car that cost $36,000 new and lists for $22,000 at four years old has kept 61.1% of its value. The fourth root of 0.611 is about 0.884, so the yearly rate is about 11.6%.

Mileage matters. AAA’s averages assume 15,000 miles a year (AAA 2026); if you drive much more, pick listings with higher miles. Plugging the value into the car lease calculator as a residual, or comparing it with a lease-end buyout price, is another use for the estimate.

Is car depreciation tax deductible?

Only for business use. The IRS says you cannot depreciate property used solely for personal activities, and for mixed use you can deduct depreciation based only on the business portion (IRS Publication 946, 2025). A car you drive only for personal errands and commuting gives you no depreciation deduction.

For business vehicles, IRS Publication 463 describes two methods: the standard mileage rate or actual expenses, which include depreciation (IRS Publication 463, 2025).

  • Standard mileage rate. 72.5 cents a mile for January 1 to June 30, 2026, and 76 cents from July 1, 2026 (IRS standard mileage rates). Of the 2026 rate, 35 cents a mile is treated as depreciation for basis purposes (IRS Notice 2026-10).
  • Actual expenses. Passenger cars face yearly depreciation caps; for 2025, $20,200 in the first year with bonus depreciation or $12,200 without (Publication 463). A car must be used more than 50% for business to qualify for accelerated methods (Publication 946).

Most employees can’t deduct unreimbursed car expenses; the IRS lists exceptions such as Armed Forces reservists, certain government officials, performing artists and eligible educators (IRS, December 29, 2025). Tax rules change; check the current publications or a tax professional for your situation.

Frequently asked questions

How do you calculate depreciation of a car?

Multiply the value at the start of each year by one minus your yearly loss rate. At 15%, a $40,000 car is worth $34,000 after one year, $28,900 after two and about $17,748 after five. The calculator repeats this for every year you plan to keep the car.

How can I estimate the depreciation of my own vehicle?

Compare what similar cars sell for today with what yours cost new. Divide today’s value by the original price, raise the result to the power of one over the car’s age in years, and subtract from one. That is your yearly rate; enter it in the tool to project future values.

How much can you write off for vehicle depreciation?

Only the business-use share counts, and passenger cars have annual caps. For 2025, the first-year cap is $20,200 with bonus depreciation or $12,200 without, per IRS Publication 463. If you use the 2026 standard mileage rate instead, 35 cents of each business mile is treated as depreciation (IRS Notice 2026-10).
